Why unfolded proteins carry more charges
In electrospray, a protein picks up protons on basic sites (lysine, arginine, histidine and the N-terminus) as the charged droplet shrinks. A compact folded protein has little surface area, so it can hold only a modest charge before the droplet’s Rayleigh limit is reached; an unfolded chain exposes many more basic sites and spreads the charge out, so it carries far more protons. More charges means lower m/z, so denaturing a protein shifts its whole envelope to lower m/z and makes it wider. Each charge state still gives the same mass after deconvolution.

What this tool shows

A mass spectrum is shaped by three things at once: the isotopes in the ion, the resolving power of the mass analyzer, and the ion’s charge. Pick Isotopes and resolution for small molecules, or Proteins by electrospray for large, multiply charged ions.

Isotope patterns

Most elements have more than one stable isotope, so a molecule exists as a mixture of isotopologues. Their relative amounts follow the binomial (multinomial) distribution. Carbon makes M+1 grow by about 1.1% per carbon; chlorine (³⁷Cl, 24%) and bromine (⁸¹Br, 49%) make big M+2 peaks, so CH₂Cl₂ shows M : M+2 : M+4 ≈ 100 : 64 : 10. The tool computes every isotopologue from IUPAC isotope masses and abundances.

Resolving power

R = m/Δm, where Δm is the peak width at half height (FWHM). At low R, all isotopologues with the same nominal mass merge into one peak. At high R, the M+1 peak splits into its fine structure (for example ¹³C vs ¹⁵N vs ²H), and isobars such as CO and C₂H₄ (both nominal 28) separate. Analyzers differ: a quadrupole keeps a constant width of about 0.7; a TOF keeps R roughly constant; Orbitrap R falls as √(m/z) and FT-ICR R as m/z.

Electrospray of proteins

A protein of mass M picks up z protons, giving peaks at m/z = (M + z·1.00728)/z. Two neighboring peaks m₁ (charge n) and m₂ (charge n + 1) give n = (m₂ − 1.00728)/(m₁ − m₂) and M = n(m₁ − 1.00728). At high resolving power each charge state splits into isotope peaks spaced 1.00235/z apart, which gives z directly. Resolving them needs R roughly equal to the protein’s mass, whatever the charge.

Isotope abundances and exact masses are IUPAC values; ions are radical cations with one electron removed. Protein isotope envelopes use the averagine composition (C4.94H7.76N1.36O1.48S0.04 per 111.1 Da). Charge-state distributions are simple models: about 0.078√M for folded proteins and roughly M/900 for unfolded ones. Peak shapes are Gaussian.
